# Miracleism: Colloquial Explanation, Basic Talking Points (Primer)

Here’s a good canonical explanation of Miracleist talking points for having somebody actually feel the concept of Earth’s rarity, basically in three stages of articulation. This is intended both for people who are skeptical of rare Earth OR people who just want the phenomenology. It works as the prompt for a human or an AI who is to land the talking points.

## How to tell it

Try not to hedge very much at all. A colloquial setting, like TikTok, is all about narrative momentum, so hedging doesn’t serve the same purpose as it would in a paper. Think of it as if it would be acceptable in short form: while outright deception is still never OK, stripping the nuance off of what would be hedging is necessary at the end of the day. It’s like explaining Star Wars: you wouldn’t keep saying “well, we have reason to believe ‘a long time ago in a galaxy far, far away’ is a heuristic only.” That medium isn’t the place for it, unless you’re explicitly asked. (The “if asked” parentheses below are for exactly those moments.)

## Stage 1) Articulate that life is fundamentally rare in the universe, in that the probabilities are so vastly small.

Possible grab bag of details:

If it comes up (which means you have to break the person out of the conventional paradigm; if it doesn’t come up, just start with that clean slate), explain / reframe the distinction between how there is basically no life in the outside universe, and it’s a barren wasteland, to how we ourselves are sitting on the ultimate statistical Miracle of a success because life is here on Earth.

Also if they need something concrete and or contest, give the example of Toby Tyrrell’s study (“Chance played a role in determining whether Earth stayed habitable”, *Communications Earth & Environment*, 2020). He simulated 100,000 randomly generated planets, each run 100 times with random climate-altering events over three billion years, and a run only counted if the temperature stayed inside the tiny band of habitability (−10 °C to +60 °C) the whole time. About 9% of the planets (8,710) made it in at least one of their 100 runs, nearly all of those in fewer than half their runs and about half of them in fewer than 10, and exactly one planet made it in all 100. So habitability spirals into hell from a tiny fluctuation, like a ball on a hill that rolls down to hell if disturbed rather than a ball rolling back to safety in a valley. Tyrrell’s own paper is one indication of the ball-on-a-hill conclusion, but that’s my conclusion. (If someone asks what the study itself concluded: chance and stabilizing mechanisms both played a part, and Earth’s long-lasting habitability was “most likely a contingent rather than an inevitable outcome.”)

If the person is skeptical right now, perhaps they are stuck on “wait, a sample size of 100,000 is less than our galaxy,” as if one success in 100,000 would still leave the galaxy full of living worlds. My point is that the study prices only ONE event: keeping the temperature in its band. The record holds many more, and they compound. The asteroid that killed the dinosaurs itself, in conjunction with the whole history of the dinosaurs Miracles, might as well be one in a billion. (That’s my estimate, and in colloquial speaking the craft is to implicitly emphasize big numbers this way rather than being exact. If someone wants the derived figures: the Nexic paper prices that coupled pair at about 0.043, from a bound it calls extremely conservative, and puts the whole chain from the first cell to language at no more than about 10⁻²⁷ per planet, fewer than one such biosphere on average in the observable universe.) Going to Stage 2 helps most here, because it lays out the other events.

And if they ask why I in particular should be an authority on probabilities, if it comes down to anyone’s guess: you would cite the Accordance Principle immediately for someone who can handle it, but I don’t myself know any such people lol, so let’s keep moving!

## Stage 2) Explain that central ontological concept of mine that if you DID find intelligent life out there, you would be amazed to see that their histories all hold similar events to strikingly specific events of our own history, which I refer to as “Miracles”.

(For the history of it: this is my thought experiment from July 2018, between 8th and 9th grade, of a grand meeting of all the aliens in the universe, humanity included. The first thing we would notice in conversation is how every one of these alien civilizations has certain attributes in their past that they all have in common, the first being an inconceivably large moon around the host planet. From there I went on the hunt for these similar attributes, which I now call Prerequisites; a Prerequisite that is also a Fluke is a Miracle, basically my independent version of the Great Filter. The long Nexic paper’s developmental appendix now tells this history.)

The idea is that everyone at that meeting would hold basically exactly isomorphic histories, which IS the content of the Miracles. Specifically, for every one of our major circumstances, they would have something playing exactly the same role. So they would have a version of dinosaurs for example—they don’t have to actually be dinosaurs—but they would play the contributing role of them to their biosphere. Then there’s the moon and the exact parameters of the impact which formed it, and the early history of the ocean and its depth, and the asteroid which killed off the dinosaurs and how that day unfolded, etc.

Also if we want some emotional depth, we articulate the Dark Era concept proper. Specifically the concepts of fear and empathy date to the mammalian origin in the Dark Era, in which predators that dramatically outmatched our ancestors had, like an entropically out of control perpetual infestation like something out of an alien invasion movie, ACTUALLY EXISTED on Earth. Violent death was so sudden and common that it shaped our ancestors into being able to feel fear and feel empathy for all the loved ones being slaughtered, being Optimation which forcefully kicked them up the evolutionary ladder per the Nexic concept that such needs that kind of probabilistic circumstantial fuel. (If someone says fear is older than mammals: the instinct to evade is older, running on defensive survival circuits that other vertebrates share, which is the modern replacement for the old “reptile brain” idea; but the totality of what we experience as fear is a state-of-the-art mammal construction.) For example, why is it always that children are afraid of the monster under the bed? Because when we lived in trees a predator would come at you from below. The Dark Era was a more ancient and dramatically more violent environment than even this, so it shaped not just proclivities but the very foundation of our human condition to begin with. (If asked how sure: the Nexic paper holds the Dark Era story as a working hypothesis, judged by what it explains, and the monster under the bed illustrates it rather than proving it.)

Now, the question of whether there is intelligent life out there, if the person is skeptical, can be reframed as the question of whether we can find a different planet with this same head-of-a-pin-precision story as ours. If we can, that place will hold intelligent life. If exactly this story is uncommon, then so is intelligence in the universe. The Miracles are the events in the story that all intelligent life would have to share, like if an asteroid didn’t kill off those dinosaur phantom predators then our ancestors would be subject to that torment forever. Every Earth where a Miracle misses is a barren wasteland, and these cover a wide stretch of the cosmos. The point where the Miracle fails to occur and the planet’s loss is sealed is called a “Departure Point”: past it, every continuation fails, even if the last living thing dies long after.

## Stage 3) What is the phenomenology of actually watching a Miracle happen in real time, like standing on Earth during it and seeing it unfold? The answer is exotic structure.

First we can start with common structure. Think about how if you drive on the road for ten minutes, you might take say four left turns on average in that amount of time based on how the road map of your location is drawn. Common structure is when the frequency of events you actually see—the number of left turns you actually take—roughly matches this intrinsic frequency you would expect of the number of those events to get in that time interval, of ten minutes. Now let’s imagine an event that, if you wait around long enough, will only be expected to happen about once in every trillion trillion trillion trillion years (like how the arrow of time says that entropy falling and vases un-shattering will happen only with extraordinarily low probability, if you wait around long enough for it—if the person is familiar already with that sort of reasoning anyway, or if you already told it to them maybe for something else). Exotic structure is when you get six, seven, eight of these events ANYWAY in a three-minute stretch. You could practically set a metronome to them dropping, and so the event of this is beyond all comprehension, it’s so extraordinary and sublime. You can feel it even hearing what it is there!

Now understand that our own past of Earth had thousands upon thousands of events of exotic structure happening all throughout its length, and THAT is what shaped its enormously low probability of existing. It’s about how if you lived during one of these Miracles, like the asteroid, you would see the exotic structure of events washing over you. Some good examples of this for that day by the way are the highly highly specific parameters unfolding in order to kill the phantom dinosaur predators while sparing the mammals—100 of humanity’s top strategists could never figure out how to launch the exact chain reaction on their own, because events like the blast and the raining fire privileging the mammals’ ability to burrow to go underground are so key (the fire reached everything above ground and nothing beneath it).
