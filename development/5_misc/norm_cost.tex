\begin{abstract}
Asked about focus, organization and initiation, a person who is late with the laundry and holds a large network of considerations in mind while building a theory is expected to report poor executive functioning. The paper starts from that case and asks under what conditions an assessment practice turns domain-limited observations into a general description of a person. It separates a function from its manifestations in a domain, a description of what someone does from an attribution of the processes involved, and both from an evaluation relative to a goal; it represents a classification scheme as the domains it samples, the indicators it uses and the reference branch it designates; and it states the Principle of Symmetry as a claim about admissible redrawings of such schemes, under which the descriptions assigned to the designated branch and to another trade places with the remainder of the branches carried along. What is established is the comparative analogue: on a fixed inventory of scored competences, each of two branches can be given a non-empty list of lower-than-the-reference traits only, by a choice of reference and inventory, exactly when the branches stand in mutual advantage at the measurement's margin, with as many traits reversing as there are competences on which the advantages cross; and when every function is compared in two domains with the comparison reversing sign between them, the descriptions trade places literally. What remains conjectural is the scheme-level symmetry under admissible redrawings, and the paper says what would ground it. A cost model is then developed as a separately testable development of the same outlook: the effort an interaction extracts is a participant baseline plus a mismatch between the participant and the conventions the interaction runs on, so that changing the conventions redistributes the effort; a within-dyad crossover is designed in which each participant's change in effort is predicted from profiles measured and norms coded in advance, and a simulation, reported in a supplement, shows what the design can and cannot establish.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{A case}

A clinician asks how a person's focus, organization and initiation are, the three things her questions gather under executive functioning. The person is late with the laundry, and understands that the expected answer is ``not great''. The same person, by his own account, holds a large network of considerations in mind at once while building a theory by inductive reasoning. The case is the author's (Appendix~\ref{app:source}), and the paper takes from it one question, which it treats as the central one: what warrants letting performance in one domain determine whether a person possesses a function in general? The questions sampled a domain, domestic activity; the description they invited was of the person. If an assessment samples some manifestations of a function and not others, its conclusion has a scope, and the scope is the domain sampled.

Three distinctions do the work of the paper, and each is drawn in \autoref{sec:symmetry}. A \emph{function} is distinguished from its \emph{manifestations}: focus shows in maintaining attention through a household task and in sustaining a question across competing explanations, and an instrument that samples one of these has not measured the other. A \emph{description} of what someone does is distinguished from an \emph{attribution} of the processes involved: that a person misses deadlines is a description; that a person lacks organization is an attribution, and the step between them is an inference whose warrant is the question just asked. And both are distinguished from an \emph{evaluation} relative to a goal, and from the \emph{assistance} a person wants, which remain real whatever the attribution: the laundry is late, and that is information the person may want acted on, without its becoming a judgement that their cognitive organization is an unsuccessful version of someone else's. The author's own account of his case, that his executive functioning has been picked up and moved into theory-building, is an explanatory hypothesis the contrast motivates and does not yet establish; the paper keeps it marked as such, and it keeps apart the clinician's questions, the author's reading of what they invited him to report, and claims about any particular standardized instrument, which the conversation did not involve.

\subsection{The argument, and what is established}

The paper develops two principles the author stated in a larger work \cite{Aethus}, in a section on the relation between cognitive style and the conventions of a dominant group; Appendix~\ref{app:source} gives the passages in full, with their provenance, and the formal apparatus, the cost model, the design and the simulation were developed in October 2026. The \emph{Branch Principle} holds that ``ontologically neurodivergent people are better understood as a collection of categorical branches and `allistic' is a single branch, such that two random branches are typically as different from one another as they each are to a third''. The \emph{Principle of Symmetry} holds that ``you can always redraw the conventional category boundaries in psychology so that allistic and a given neurodivergent branch `trade places phenomenologically'{}''. The paper's reading of the second is that the descriptive content of a diagnosis is a description assigned by a classification scheme, a scheme being the domains it samples, the indicators it uses for each function and the branch it designates as reference, and that the principle asserts the existence, for the designated branch and any other, of an admissible redrawing of the scheme under which the two descriptions trade places, the remainder of the branches carried along with the designation. ``Admissible'' is where the content lies: with unrestricted reassignment of indicators the trading of places is satisfiable by definition, and the paper states the constraints that make it a claim about cognition.

What the paper establishes and what it leaves conjectural are stated apart. Established is the comparative analogue (\autoref{prop:reversibility}): on a fixed inventory of scored competences, each of two branches can be given a non-empty list of lower-than-the-reference traits only, by a choice of reference and inventory, exactly when the two branches stand in mutual advantage at the measurement's margin, and the number of traits that can be made to reverse is the number of competences on which the advantages cross, no more; and the descriptions trade places literally, in the sense of \eqref{eq:trade}, when every function is compared in two domains and the comparison reverses sign between them (\autoref{cor:literal}). Conjectural is the scheme-level symmetry under admissible redrawings, of which the comparative result is the analogue in which a redrawing is a choice of which already-scored competences to sample; what would ground it, function by function, is a correspondence between manifestations across domains with independent evidence behind it, and the executive-function literature already holds some of the materials (\autoref{sec:established}). Reference-relativity itself is definitional, ``roughly half-and-half'' is a conjecture nothing rests on, and the remark that autistic people ``are described as taking a script to allistic conversations'', when ``if the shoe were on the other foot'' allistic people would be, is the seed of the paper's second part.

\subsection{A separately testable development}

If descriptions are scheme-relative, the difficulty of an interaction between differently situated people can be asked about in the relation between a person and the conventions the interaction runs on, rather than in the person. The double empathy problem \cite{Milton2012} already relocated the difficulty from the autistic person to the pair, a mutual failure of understanding between people whose ways of experiencing and communicating differ, with a decade of empirical work behind it \cite{Milton2022, DavisCrompton2021, Pellicano2022}; Milton's account also has the conventions in view, social subtext being actively constructed and a label that locates the problem in the other resolving the one who applies it. What the accounts examined here do not have is the conventions as a measured and manipulable variable with a prediction of how a change in them redistributes effort between the partners. The paper's second part supplies that as a hypothesis independent of the first part, which motivates and does not entail it: a cost model in which effort is a participant baseline plus a mismatch between the participant's communicative style and the style the governing norms presuppose, plus a one-sided shortfall against the competence the norms require, with a within-dyad crossover that predicts each participant's change in effort from profiles measured and norms coded in advance, against the rivals of a general protocol benefit and partner mismatch. Interactions between differently situated people have a theoretical setting in aptitude-by-treatment interactions \cite{Cronbach1957, CronbachSnow1977}; the double-empathy literature has a formal model of channel preference \cite{Calderoli2026}; what is added is the prediction, from independently measured profiles and independently coded rules, of who bears the cost of adaptation when the rules change. The simulation that shows what the design can and cannot establish is reported in a supplement and summarized in \autoref{sec:simulation}.

Two commitments govern the writing: the scope rule of \autoref{sec:labels} for every claim about ``the autistic'', and the recommendations to autism researchers on ableist language \cite{BottemaBeutel2021}, so that ``autistic'' and ``non-autistic'' are the groups' names, ``allistic'' appears only inside the source's quotations, and no competence difference is called a deficit: the paper's vocabulary is descriptions, attributions and comparative traits relative to a stated reference, and ``deficit'' and ``impairment'' appear only where the clinical literature's own usage is reported.

\section{A label as a partial information state}
\label{sec:labels}

A diagnostic label carries what it records and no more. The larger framework calls such a state an Aethus and any fuller description of the same person a proper child of it \cite{Aethus}; this paper says \emph{partial information state} and \emph{refinement}. The point of the vocabulary is not metaphysical. It is that a label is an object with a definite content, and that a claim about everyone who bears it is licensed by that content or not at all.

Three kinds of claim about the bearers of a label are therefore distinguished.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Definitional entailment.} What the label records holds of every bearer. The diagnostic criteria for autism spectrum disorder in the fifth edition of the \emph{Diagnostic and Statistical Manual} require all three of the social-communication features and at least two of the four restricted and repetitive features, currently or by history \cite{APA2013}. So ``every bearer shows at least two of the four'' is entailed. ``Every bearer has sensory differences'' is not: sensory reactivity is one of the four, and the label does not record which two.
\item \emph{Group-level statistical prediction.} A claim about the distribution of bearers. It is true or false of the group, and attributing it to each member is the ecological fallacy \cite{Robinson1950}.
\item \emph{Individual attribution.} A claim about a particular bearer, which needs information about that bearer.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}[Scope rule]
\label{def:scope}
A claim about every bearer of a label is licensed in two ways only: the label's definition entails it, or independent evidence establishes it for every bearer. A claim that needs information the label does not carry is made of a refinement that carries it, or not made.
\end{definition}

\noindent The rule is the discipline the rest of the paper is written under. It does not forbid universal claims; it says where they come from.

\paragraph{The source's illustration.} The section the principles come from gives one worked instance of a claim that violates the rule (Appendix~\ref{app:source}): for any trait some autistic branch can be found on which it presents as infantile, which seems to confirm that ``autistic people are infantile in this way''. Two errors are in play. With $I(b, \tau)$ reading ``$\tau$ presents as infantile on $b$ relative to a chosen reference branch'' and $\mathcal{B}$ the branches the label collects, the category-to-individual step $\exists b \in \mathcal{B}\; I(b, \tau) \not\Rightarrow \forall b \in \mathcal{B}\; I(b, \tau)$ is the scope rule's violation, and the interchange of $\forall \tau\, \exists b\; I(b, \tau)$ with $\exists b\, \forall \tau\; I(b, \tau)$ is what makes the collected witnesses look like one profile. The source's random-partition control shows that the confirmations depend on the partition chosen, the reference class problem \cite{Hajek2007} in categorical form, with the qualification that an arbitrary partition need not place a witness for every trait in the half containing the allistic branch.

\section{Classification schemes and the Principle of Symmetry}
\label{sec:symmetry}

\subsection{The executive-functioning case, taken apart}
\label{sec:ef-case}

The case of \autoref{sec:intro} has a family of functions, executive functioning, three functions the clinician's questions gather under it, focus, organization and initiation, and two domains in which each might show. Table~\ref{tab:manifestations} sets the domains side by side. Both columns list candidate manifestations, the right-hand one activities that would count as the function's showing in theoretical work if the correspondence between the columns holds, not activities already shown to engage the same processes as the left-hand ones.

The inference the case questions runs from the left-hand column to the person: performance in the sampled domain is taken to settle whether the person has the function. Its warrant is the assumption that the sampled manifestations are representative of the function's manifestations in general, and that assumption is what the right-hand column puts in doubt. The research vocabulary does not exclude the right-hand column: reasoning, problem-solving and planning are counted among the higher-order executive functions in the standard review \cite{Diamond2013}, so the recognition of executive activity in theoretical work is already available to the field. And the measurement literature has the corresponding fact: across twenty studies, performance-based measures of executive function and ratings of it correlated with a median of $.19$, and their reviewers concluded that the two kinds of measure capture different aspects or levels of cognition \cite{Toplak2013}. That fact does not establish any symmetry between branches. It establishes that attributions of the same function from different samples of its manifestations need not agree, which is the premise the case needs for its charge: a scope-limited conclusion presented as a general one.

The objection the case makes is therefore not that everyone has strengths and weaknesses, nor that the field has no visibility into reasoning or context. It is a claim about the relation among a concept, its selected indicators and the person described through them: an assessment that samples some manifestations and reports a general attribution has drawn a conclusion with a scope it did not state. The author's further account, that the capacities have been ``picked up and moved'' into theory-building, is an explanatory hypothesis about one person, which the contrast motivates and which would need its own evidence; the paper uses the case for the question, not for the hypothesis. The historical and political objections the author raises to the deficit reading of the categories motivate the project and are not argued here; claims about the origins of diagnostic categories need their own evidence, and the measurement argument stands without them.

\subsection{Classification schemes and admissible redrawings}
\label{sec:schemes}

The descriptive content of a diagnosis is a description assigned by a scheme, and the scheme can be written down.

\begin{definition}[Classification scheme]
\label{def:scheme}
A classification scheme $\Sigma$ consists of a finite set of branches $\mathcal{B}$ with a designated reference branch $r_\Sigma \in \mathcal{B}$, whose complement $\mathcal{B} \setminus \{r_\Sigma\}$ is the remainder the scheme's labels collect; a set $\Phi$ of functions it attributes; for each $f \in \Phi$ a sampled domain $\Delta_\Sigma(f)$ of activities and an indicator rule that maps a branch's performance in $\Delta_\Sigma(f)$ to a trait description, a rule that may or may not read the performance against $r_\Sigma$ at a stated margin; and the category boundaries the labels draw on $\mathcal{B}$. The description $F_\Sigma(b)$ is the list of trait descriptions the scheme assigns to branch $b$ over $\Phi$.
\end{definition}

\noindent Whether a scheme's traits are comparative in disguise, whether ``poor organization'' is ``lower than the reference on organization in the sampled domain'', is a question about its indicator rules, answered scheme by scheme; the comparative result of \autoref{sec:comparative} treats the rules that are, and nothing here requires that every trait be. A \emph{redrawing} of $\Sigma$ is a scheme $\Sigma'$ together with a bijection $\pi$ of $\mathcal{B}$ that exchanges $r_\Sigma$ with a branch $b$, with $r_{\Sigma'} = b$, and so carries the remainder $\mathcal{B} \setminus \{r_\Sigma\}$ to $\mathcal{B} \setminus \{b\}$; this is the structure the source insists on, that the exchange is between a single branch and the collection of the remaining ones, never between ``allistic'' and ``autistic'' as if the second were a branch, which the Branch Principle counts a category error (Appendix~\ref{app:source}). The redrawing \emph{trades places} for the pair when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:trade}
F_{\Sigma'}(\pi(x)) \;=\; F_\Sigma(x) \qquad \text{for } x \in \{r_\Sigma, b\},
\end{equation}
the description the old scheme gave the reference being the one the new scheme gives $b$, and conversely. Equation~\eqref{eq:trade} is satisfiable by definition if $\Sigma'$ may reassign indicators at will, and it then says nothing about cognition. The content lies in what redrawings are admitted.

\begin{definition}[Admissible redrawing]
\label{def:admissible}
A redrawing $(\Sigma', \pi)$ of $\Sigma$ is admissible when
\begin{enumerate}[label=(A\arabic*), leftmargin=*, nosep]
\item it attributes the same functions, $\Phi_{\Sigma'} = \Phi_\Sigma$;
\item for each function $f$ the new sampled domain $\Delta_{\Sigma'}(f)$ consists of activities that are manifestations of $f$ under a correspondence stated independently of the outcome, which says why an activity in the new domain and one in the old are the same function at work, what differences between them are held not to matter, and on what evidence;
\item the indicator rules are applied with the standards of the old scheme, the same margin and the same reliability requirements, read against the new reference.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\noindent Under (A2) the table of \autoref{sec:ef-case} is a proposed correspondence for one function, and the admissibility of the redrawing that samples theoretical work instead of domestic activity turns on whether the correspondence can be argued and evidenced: whether organizing an investigation and organizing a household are organization in the same sense, what differences between them matter, and what evidence supports the exchanged descriptions. Those constraints are what make a symmetry informative.

\begin{conjecture}[The Principle of Symmetry, scheme form]
\label{conj:scheme-symmetry}
For a scheme $\Sigma$ with designated branch $r_\Sigma$ and any other branch $b$ there is an admissible redrawing $(\Sigma', \pi)$ that trades places for the pair, \eqref{eq:trade}.
\end{conjecture}

\noindent The conjecture is the paper's reading of the source's ``you can always redraw the conventional category boundaries'', and it is stated as a conjecture because its truth depends, function by function, on correspondences that have to be established. Its comparative analogue is proved below: when the admissible redrawings are the choices of which already-scored competences to sample, with a competence allowed to be a function in a domain, the trading of comparative trait lists holds exactly under a stated and measurable condition, and \eqref{eq:trade} itself holds literally under a stronger, paired condition stated there.

\subsection{Profiles, references and trait lists}
\label{sec:comparative}

Let $T$ be a finite inventory of competences and let each branch $b$ have a profile $x_b \colon T \to \mathbb{R}$, a score on each competence on a common scale. The scale carries a measurement margin $\delta \ge 0$, the smallest difference the instrument resolves or the analyst is prepared to call a difference.

\begin{definition}[Comparative traits and trait lists]
\label{def:deficit}
For each competence $\tau$ there are two comparative traits, $\tau^{-}$, \emph{lower on $\tau$ than the reference}, and $\tau^{+}$, \emph{higher on $\tau$ than the reference}. Relative to a reference branch $r$ at margin $\delta$, branch $b$ has the trait $\tau^{-}$ when $x_b(\tau) < x_r(\tau) - \delta$ and the trait $\tau^{+}$ when $x_b(\tau) > x_r(\tau) + \delta$; write
\[ D_r(b;\delta) \;=\; \{\, \tau \in T : x_b(\tau) < x_r(\tau) - \delta \,\}, \qquad U_r(b;\delta) \;=\; \{\, \tau \in T : x_b(\tau) > x_r(\tau) + \delta \,\} \]
for the competences on which $b$ is lower and higher than $r$. The \emph{trait list} of $b$ relative to $r$ on an inventory $S \subseteq T$ is $L_r(b; S) = \{\tau^{-} : \tau \in S \cap D_r(b;\delta)\} \cup \{\tau^{+} : \tau \in S \cap U_r(b;\delta)\}$.
\end{definition}

\noindent A trait list is what a diagnosis attributes on a fixed inventory, once the evaluation is stripped from it; ``deficit'' is the reference's reading of $\tau^{-}$, and nothing below uses it. The inventory is the scheme's choice of what to sample (\autoref{sec:schemes}): a competence here may be a function in a domain, focus in domestic activity and focus in theoretical work being two competences, so that a redrawing of the sampled domains is a change of inventory. Three statements follow, of three different kinds.

\paragraph{(S1) Reference-relativity, a definition.} A comparative trait is a three-place relation among a branch, a reference and a competence, and $L_r(b; S)$ has no reference-free extension: there is no comparative list of this kind until $r$ is fixed. The claim is about comparative traits and not about every description, since ``uses written reminders'' names no reference; whether the traits a scheme attributes are comparative in disguise is the question of \autoref{sec:schemes}, not something the definition settles. The identity $D_r(b;\delta) = U_b(r;\delta)$ holds exactly and for any two profiles, the lower traits of one branch being the higher traits of the other: that sign reversal needs no condition, and it is not the reversal the Principle of Symmetry asserts, since a branch higher on every competence receives only higher traits relative to the other however the reference is set. Choosing another reference makes another comparison, not a correction of the first. This is the content of the source's formalization, ``the reference-relativity being the crux rather than a modeling convenience''.

\paragraph{(S2) Reversibility at margin $\delta$, an exact consequence.}
\begin{proposition}[Reversibility of trait lists]
\label{prop:reversibility}
For branches $b$ and $r$ and margin $\delta \ge 0$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=*, nosep]
\item there are inventories $S$ and $S'$ on which $b$'s list relative to $r$ consists of lower traits only, $L_r(b; S) = \{\tau^{-} : \tau \in S\} \neq \emptyset$, and $r$'s list relative to $b$ consists of lower traits only, $L_b(r; S') = \{\sigma^{-} : \sigma \in S'\} \neq \emptyset$;
\item there are competences $\tau$ and $\sigma$ with $x_b(\tau) - x_r(\tau) > \delta$ and $x_r(\sigma) - x_b(\sigma) > \delta$.
\end{enumerate}
Call (b) \emph{$\delta$-mutual advantage}. Whether or not it holds, for any $S^{-} \subseteq D_r(b;\delta)$ and $S^{+} \subseteq U_r(b;\delta)$ the inventory $S^{-} \cup S^{+}$ gives $b$ the list $\{\tau^{-} : \tau \in S^{-}\} \cup \{\tau^{+} : \tau \in S^{+}\}$ and $r$ the mirror list; what mutual advantage adds is that both $D_r(b;\delta)$ and $U_r(b;\delta)$ are non-empty, so that each branch can be given a list of lower traits only. The number of lower traits $b$ can be given is at most $|D_r(b;\delta)|$ and the number of higher traits at most $|U_r(b;\delta)|$; a construction with three of each needs both sets to hold at least three competences, and a finite inventory supports no finer reversal than its own size.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(a) says that $D_r(b;\delta)$ and $D_b(r;\delta)$ are both non-empty, and $D_b(r;\delta) = U_r(b;\delta)$. $\sigma \in D_r(b;\delta)$ is $x_r(\sigma) - x_b(\sigma) > \delta$, and $\tau \in U_r(b;\delta)$ is $x_b(\tau) - x_r(\tau) > \delta$, which is (b). The remaining clauses are the definition of the lists read on $S^{-} \cup S^{+}$, with $L_b(r; S)$ the sign-reversed image of $L_r(b; S)$ for every $S$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Literal trading of places]
\label{cor:literal}
Let each function $f \in \Phi$ be scored in two domains, so that $(f, 0)$ and $(f, 1)$ are competences, and let $\Sigma$ sample domain $0$ with reference $r$ and $\Sigma'$ sample domain $1$ with reference $b$, both reading comparisons at margin $\delta$, the reference branch receiving the empty list. If for every $f$ the comparison of $b$ with $r$ reverses sign between the domains, $b$ lower than $r$ by more than $\delta$ in one and higher by more than $\delta$ in the other, then $F_{\Sigma'}(\pi(x)) = F_\Sigma(x)$ for $x \in \{r, b\}$: the descriptions trade places literally.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
$F_\Sigma(r) = \emptyset = F_{\Sigma'}(b)$, the reference receiving the empty list in each scheme. For each $f$, $F_\Sigma(b)$ holds $f^{-}$ exactly when $x_b(f,0) < x_r(f,0) - \delta$, and $F_{\Sigma'}(r)$ holds $f^{-}$ exactly when $x_r(f,1) < x_b(f,1) - \delta$, which under the sign reversal is the same condition; likewise for $f^{+}$. So $F_{\Sigma'}(r) = F_\Sigma(b)$.
\end{proof}

\noindent The corollary's condition is stronger than $\delta$-mutual advantage: it asks for a crossed comparison within every function, across its two domains, where mutual advantage asks for one competence each way. It is the condition Table~\ref{tab:manifestations} would have to meet for executive functioning, and whether the redrawing it uses is admissible is (A2)'s question, not the corollary's.

\noindent Under $\delta$-mutual advantage, then, the ``trade places'' of the Principle of Symmetry (\autoref{sec:intro}), the comparative analogue of \autoref{conj:scheme-symmetry}, holds in this sense and in this sense only, of the comparative traits attributed on a fixed inventory: on the inventory $D_r(b;\delta)$ every trait of $b$ is a lower trait and every trait of $r$ a higher one, on $U_r(b;\delta)$ the reverse, and the author's six traits are the case $|S^{-}| = |S^{+}| = 3$, available when each set holds three competences. It holds exactly when the premise holds and fails exactly when it does not. The result compares two profiles and says nothing of the other branches; the designation of a reference and the remainder it leaves are the scheme's, and \autoref{sec:schemes} carries them. Two remarks fix the premise.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Non-domination is not enough above $\delta = 0$.} At $\delta = 0$, (b) is the statement that neither profile is at or above the other on every competence. At a positive margin it is stronger. Take $T = \{\tau_1, \tau_2\}$, $x_b = (1.05, 1)$, $x_r = (1, 1.05)$ and $\delta = 0.1$: neither profile dominates, yet $D_r(b;\delta)$ and $U_r(b;\delta)$ are both empty, and no inventory attributes a trait to either.
\item \emph{What is testable.} Whether a non-reference branch exceeds the reference somewhere by more than the margin is a question about measured profiles, and it is answered competence by competence. The source's own case, two competences at opposite developmental ages (Appendix~\ref{app:source}), illustrates the premise's shape; it is offered as motivation and not as a measurement on commensurate scales, and it carries no evidential weight here.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{(S3) Balance, a conjecture.}
\begin{conjecture}[Balance]
\label{conj:balance}
For the reference branch and a given non-reference branch, the sets $D_r(b;\delta)$ and $U_r(b;\delta)$ carry comparable weight under a natural weighting of the competences.
\end{conjecture}
\noindent This is the source's ``roughly half-and-half''. It needs a weighting of competences, and no weighting is natural until the inventory's granularity is settled, since splitting one competence into five multiplies its weight; the paper draws nothing from it.

\subsection{What is established and what is conjectural}
\label{sec:established}

Three things are now in view, and the paper names which is which.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*, nosep]
\item \emph{Established.} On a fixed inventory of scored competences, each of two branches can be given a non-empty list of lower traits only, by a choice of reference and inventory, exactly when the branches stand in $\delta$-mutual advantage, and the reversal extends to as many traits as there are competences on which the advantages cross, $|D_r(b;\delta)|$ lower and $|U_r(b;\delta)|$ higher, and no further (\autoref{prop:reversibility}); and the descriptions trade places literally when every function's comparison reverses sign between two sampled domains (\autoref{cor:literal}). This is the comparative analogue of \autoref{conj:scheme-symmetry}, a redrawing being a choice of what to sample among competences already scored on a common scale.
\item \emph{Conjectural.} The scheme-level symmetry, \autoref{conj:scheme-symmetry}, for the functions clinical schemes attribute. What would ground it, for executive functioning, is the correspondence of Table~\ref{tab:manifestations} argued under (A2) and evidenced: that the right-hand activities engage the function the left-hand ones do, with the differences that matter named. The low agreement between performance-based measures and ratings \cite{Toplak2013} shows that attributions from different manifestations diverge, which the conjecture requires, and does not show that they diverge in the mirrored way the conjecture asserts.
\item \emph{Not claimed.} That the populations have no structure of their own, or that clinical diagnoses, with their requirements of history and impairment, trade places; these are the claims (ii) and (iii) of the next subsection.
\end{enumerate}
The paper's own case for the autistic--non-autistic pair is that the established condition is satisfied on the competences the study measures, which the premeasured profiles of \autoref{sec:design} can check on their inventory, though nothing in the cost model's test depends on it (\autoref{sec:not-premise}).

\subsection{The clinic illustration, and four claims kept apart}
\label{sec:redrawing}

The illustration is stipulated. Consider a clinic whose reference population is a branch of autistic adults and whose diagnostic manual was written from their norms, and read its intake notes on one hypothetical client from the majority.
\begin{quote}
The client seeks sustained eye contact during speech and reports discomfort when it is not returned. Requests are seldom stated; the client encodes them as hints, expects the listener to reconstruct them, and is frustrated when the literal content is acted on. Attention moves between topics within a session, and the client reports discomfort when held to one. In group settings the client engages in repetitive rhythmic whole-body movement to music, and does not experience it as stereotyped.
\end{quote}
Four behaviours, each stipulated of this client and none attributed to the majority as a whole, an attribution the scope rule of \autoref{sec:labels} forbids. What the notes do is describe each in the register the majority's manual keeps for the minority: dancing written up as stimming; a breadth of attention as the inverse of monotropism, the attention tunnel of few interests highly aroused \cite{Murray2005}; reliance on implicature as a difficulty with literal content. A reader who, with the heading covered, finds the notes plausible as a description of an autistic client has felt the point from the inside, and the point is about the evaluative work the clinical register does. The illustration is a redrawing of \autoref{sec:schemes} with the reference moved and the indicators kept, and it separates the behaviour, its function for the person, its description, and whether it impairs: the register fixes the third and invites an inference to the fourth that the description alone does not establish, and the illustration shows the third moving while the first two stay put. The source's own first version of the experiment, in 2024, ran the inversion on ``logical'' against ``nuanced'' (Appendix~\ref{app:source}). Four claims are then to be kept apart, since the illustration invites running them together.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*, nosep]
\item Each of two branches can be given a list of lower traits only, by a change of reference and inventory. This is proved, under the stated condition.
\item An autistic and a non-autistic person's clinical diagnoses trade places. This needs more than (i): a diagnosis under the criteria the paper cites requires a developmental history and clinically significant impairment beside the behavioural features \cite{APA2013}, and rewriting behaviour in clinical language does not establish that those are met. Whether those requirements are themselves reference-relative is a further argument, not made here.
\item The diagnostic distinction has no basis in the structure of the populations. This does not follow from (i):\footnote{Two populations clustered tightly at competence profiles $(10, 1)$ and $(1, 10)$ satisfy the condition, and selecting either competence reverses which appears lower, while the populations remain sharply distinguishable types with different causes.} reference-relative ranking and the structure of the population are different questions, and the second is the Branch Principle's, outside this paper (\autoref{sec:extensions}), where the source's comparison with the pre-Darwinian division of humans from animals belongs (Appendix~\ref{app:source}).
\item Changing the governing norms redistributes effort. This is an independent empirical hypothesis, the cost model of \autoref{sec:model}. A world with crossed competence advantages and no effort response to a change of norms is consistent with (i).
\end{enumerate}
What the experiment establishes is (i); what it motivates is (iv): if the traits by which the divide is drawn are reference-relative, the cost of a mixed interaction can be asked about in the relation between a person and the conventions the interaction runs on, and conventions can be changed in a laboratory. The rest of the paper asks that question; the experiment does not answer it.


\subsection{Difficulties without deficiency}
\label{sec:difficulties}

Purging the deficit reading does not purge difficulty. The paper keeps four things apart and keeps all four: a description of what someone does; an attribution of the processes involved; an evaluation relative to a particular goal; and the assistance or environmental change the person wants. Someone's difficulty completing an activity they want completed is information, and it stays information under every redrawing; what the redrawing removes is the step from the description to a judgement that the person's branch is an unsuccessful version of another. The cost model of \autoref{sec:model} is built to this discipline. Its competence requirement belongs to a specified protocol, not to a branch, and a shortfall against it is a relation between a person and a task; the model never carries the requirement from the protocol to the person, and the paper keeps the requirement attached to the protocol wherever the term appears.

\subsection{Why mutual advantage is not a premise of what follows}
\label{sec:not-premise}

The cost model of \autoref{sec:model} predicts that a change of norms moves effort whether or not the two groups stand in $\delta$-mutual advantage: a participant can gain from a change of norms while scoring lower on every measured competence, if the new norms presuppose less of what that participant scores lower on, and a participant who exceeds the reference somewhere gains nothing from norms that do not draw on it. The principles motivate the comparison the model makes; they are not its premises, and the model's test does not test them, which lets the empirical work proceed without settling the geometry of cognitive profiles (\autoref{sec:extensions}).

\section{Context sensitivity: what toggling shows}
\label{sec:toggle}

The source makes one further observation, in a different section and about a different convention, which bears on how any difference between groups should be read: ``A limitation does not toggle.'' An adult who switches between two modes of a convention within a day, by context, shows that ``the convention is a mode the platform runs, selectable, not a ceiling the platform imposes'' (the passage is in Appendix~\ref{app:source}). The observation is stated for one person switching between two modes by context, and against one rival, a fixed limitation. Against that rival it is decisive, and the paper takes exactly that much from it.

\begin{proposition}[What a toggle refutes]
\label{prop:toggle}
Let $P$ be a performance and $C$ a set of conditions. If $P$ is observed under some $c \in C$, the claim that the person cannot produce $P$ under any condition in $C$ is false.
\end{proposition}
\noindent The proposition is immediate, and its value is in what it does not say: persistence does not identify capacity, since a convention can persist across every instrument that shares its assumptions, and change does not identify convention, since a stable capacity yields different performance under different demands. The toggle is a test of \emph{context sensitivity}. A change refutes the unconditional claim of incapacity and opens a question of mechanism, which further experiments answer by varying demand, time, load and format separately from the governing norm. In the study of \autoref{sec:design} the two conditions differ in the governing norm, are matched on specified incidental features, and have their remaining differences in training and practice measured, which is what lets a change be read as the norm's up to those measured differences.

Applying the observation to differences between groups is this paper's extension, and it is labelled as such. The participant-specific baseline $a_i$ of the model below is accordingly a baseline over the conditions studied and not a measure of capacity, and the components of it that might vary with the conditions, sensory load and fatigue, are held fixed or measured in the design.

\section{The cost of interaction under governing norms}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Profiles and norms}

Each participant $i$ has two profiles, both measured before any interaction.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A \emph{style profile} $s_i \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$: the participant's communicative preferences on dimensions such as directness, literalness, turn-taking and channel, scored on common scales.
\item A \emph{competence profile} $k_i \colon T \to \mathbb{R}$ over the abilities the interaction draws on, the profile of \autoref{sec:symmetry} measured for individuals.
\end{itemize}
A governing norm $n$, a rule set under which the interaction runs, fixes two things.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item A \emph{presupposed style} $s^{*}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$: the communicator the rules are written for, coded from the rules' text by raters who do not see the outcomes.
\item A \emph{competence requirement} $\theta_n \colon T \to \mathbb{R}$ with weights $w_n \colon T \to [0, 1]$: how much of each competence the rules call on, and how heavily.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The cost model}

\begin{hypothesis}[The cost model]
\label{hyp:cost}
Participant $i$'s effort under norm $n$ is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cost}
c_i(n) \;=\; a_i \;+\; m_i(n) \;+\; \varepsilon_i(n), \qquad
m_i(n) \;=\; \lambda_s\, d\big(s_i, s^{*}_n\big) \;+\; \lambda_k \sum_{\tau \in T} w_n(\tau)\, \pos{\theta_n(\tau) - k_i(\tau)},
\end{equation}
with $\pos{u} = \max(0, u)$, $d$ a distance on style profiles, $\lambda_s, \lambda_k \ge 0$, $a_i$ a participant-specific baseline over the conditions studied and $\varepsilon_i(n)$ zero-mean noise.
\end{hypothesis}

\noindent The two terms of the mismatch $m_i(n)$ have different shapes, and the difference is the point.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{The style term is symmetric.} Departing from the presupposed style in either direction costs effort: a direct communicator under indirect rules and an indirect one under direct rules both adapt. A distance is the right form for a mismatch of styles, where neither pole is better.
\item \emph{The competence term is one-sided.} Falling short of a requirement costs effort; exceeding it costs nothing. A participant whose competence exceeds what the rules call on is not penalized for the excess, which a symmetric distance on competences would wrongly do.
\item \emph{The baseline is a baseline.} $a_i$ collects whatever is constant across the conditions studied: capacity, in part, but also sensory load, fatigue and anything else the conditions share. It is not identified as capacity, and the model does not call it that.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Status.} Equation~\eqref{eq:cost} is a hypothesis about effort, which the paper tests. Reference-relativity, (S1), makes the comparison between a participant and a norm well defined, the norm's requirement being the reference against which the one-sided term is read; the hypothesis says how effort tracks it. Neither the Branch Principle nor reference-relativity entails the equation, and nothing in the framework it comes from does either.

\paragraph{Outcomes.} Effort is the primary outcome, a subjective workload score per participant and condition (\autoref{sec:design}); task performance and rapport are secondary, each with a model of its own, since the model does not derive rapport from costs and ``depends on'' yields no prediction.

\subsection{The predictions, exactly}
\label{sec:predictions}

Write $G(n)$ for the expected group gap in effort under norm $n$, the minority group's mean less the majority group's,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gap}
G(n) \;=\; \Delta a \;+\; \Delta M(n),
\end{equation}
with $\Delta a$ the baseline gap and $\Delta M(n)$ the gap in mean mismatch, both signed in the same direction.

\paragraph{P1, the gap under the default norms $n_0$.} $G(n_0) = \Delta a + \Delta M(n_0)$. Under norms that presuppose the majority's style and draw on its competences, $\Delta M(n_0) > 0$ whenever the minority's profiles lie farther from $s^{*}_{n_0}$ and fall shorter of $\theta_{n_0}$, and the gap is the sum of two parts that the default condition cannot separate.

\paragraph{P2, norm sensitivity, the primary prediction.} For a second norm $n_1$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:p2-group}
G(n_1) - G(n_0) \;=\; \Delta M(n_1) - \Delta M(n_0),
\end{equation}
whatever $\Delta a$ is; and within each person the baseline cancels,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:p2-within}
c_i(n_1) - c_i(n_0) \;=\; m_i(n_1) - m_i(n_0) \;+\; \varepsilon_i(n_1) - \varepsilon_i(n_0),
\end{equation}
so that a participant's change in effort is predicted, up to noise, by the change in a quantity measured before the interaction. Equation~\eqref{eq:p2-within} is the strongest test the model offers, because it runs on premeasured profiles, person by person, and because the restrictions $M_\gamma$ and $M_\mu$ of \autoref{sec:family} predict a slope of zero for it.

\paragraph{P3, reversal, the strong form.}
\begin{proposition}[The reversal condition]
\label{prop:reversal}
Suppose $G(n_0) > 0$. The gap reverses under $n_1$, $G(n_1) < 0$, exactly when either of the following equivalent conditions holds:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=*, nosep]
\item the norm-induced reduction exceeds the initial total gap, $\Delta M(n_0) - \Delta M(n_1) > G(n_0)$;
\item under the new norms the mismatch term favours the minority by more than the baseline gap, $-\Delta M(n_1) > \Delta a$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:gap}, $G(n_1) = G(n_0) - [\Delta M(n_0) - \Delta M(n_1)]$, which is negative exactly under (a); and $G(n_1) = \Delta a + \Delta M(n_1)$, which is negative exactly under (b).
\end{proof}

\noindent The condition is stricter than the natural reading of ``the norm effect outweighs the baseline''. Take $\Delta a = 3$ and let the mismatch contribution move from $+2$ under $n_0$ to $-2$ under $n_1$. The reduction is $4$, larger than the baseline gap, but smaller than $G(n_0) = 5$: the gap falls from $5$ to $1$ and does not reverse. Reversal needs a reduction greater than $5$, equivalently a new mismatch contribution below $-3$; Figure~\ref{fig:reversal} draws the arithmetic. In the language of aptitude-by-treatment interaction, P2 is an interaction of group with condition, and P3 is the condition for it to be disordinal, the groups' lines crossing between $n_0$ and $n_1$.

\section{The model family, and the test that separates its members}
\label{sec:family}

\subsection{One model, four restrictions}

Add to \eqref{eq:cost} the two terms a rival would supply: a condition effect $\gamma_n$ common to all participants, and a mismatch with the partner. For participant $i$ with partner $j$ under norm $n$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:family}
c_i(n, j) \;=\; a_i \;+\; \gamma_n \;+\; m_i(n) \;+\; \mu\, d\big(s_i, s_j\big) \;+\; \varepsilon_i(n).
\end{equation}
The models compared are restrictions of \eqref{eq:family}, and each makes a definite prediction for a change of rule set with the partners held fixed (Table~\ref{tab:family}).

\noindent No tradition is identified with a restriction. An account that emphasizes individual limitations can predict effects of task demands and of accommodations, and so is not $M_\gamma$ or $M_\mu$ by fiat; a double-empathy account can include norm effects, and so is not $M_\mu$ by fiat. The restrictions are compared as models, by what they predict for the data of \autoref{sec:design}.

\subsection{The double-empathy model, read from its text}

The formal model the double-empathy literature has is the distinct communication strategies model \cite{Calderoli2026}, and its characterization here is taken from the paper's own text rather than from its abstract.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{The implemented mechanism} is channel preference. Each agent registers the partner's empathy as a weighted average of the partner's verbal and nonverbal output, with the verbal weight the agent's own preference $cp$; the gap between the empathy the agent expects and the empathy it registers raises its defensiveness, which lowers its own output, and the loop can collapse.
\item \emph{What the text allows.} It states that expectations ``may depend on context, relationship history, communication norms'', while holding expected empathy at one value for both agents in its simulations; it predicts that ``the partner with higher $cp$ should report lower perceived empathy''; and its limitations section says that ``the costs are therefore bilateral but need not be equal or arise from the same source''.
\item \emph{What it has no parameter for.} An imposed or governing norm. Its intervention prediction concerns training that increases verbal empathy expression under stress.
\end{itemize}
It would therefore be wrong to say that this model predicts equal costs, or invariance under a change of norms; it is silent on norms as implemented, and open to them in its text. The distinction between it and the norm-setting model has to be shown in data, and the data can show it only under assumptions, which are stated next.

\paragraph{The mediation analysis, and what it can establish.} Suppose the double-empathy mechanism is extended so that expected empathy depends on the governing norm. A change of rule set then acts on effort \emph{through} the gap between the empathy a participant expects and the empathy the participant perceives: the gap moves, and effort moves with it. Under $M_\lambda$ effort moves with premeasured profile-to-norm mismatch whether or not the gap moves. Three things follow for the design, and together they are less than a test.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{The mediator is the gap, not perceived empathy.} Expected empathy may change with the norm while perceived empathy does not, and the whole change in effort may then run through the gap; an analysis that controlled perceived empathy alone would count it as a norm effect beside the mechanism.\footnote{Under rule set A a participant expects $8$ and perceives $6$; under B expects $6$ and perceives $6$: the gap falls from $2$ to $0$ with perceived empathy unmoved.} The design therefore measures both, expected partner empathy before each condition's task and perceived after it, and the mediator is their difference.
\item \emph{Controlling the mediator does not identify the direct effect by design.} The rule set is randomized; the gap is not. The coefficient of premeasured mismatch with the gap's change controlled is the direct effect only under sequential ignorability \cite{ImaiKeeleTingley2010, ImaiKeeleYamamoto2010}, which measurement error in the reports, common causes of the reports and of effort, and feedback from effort to the judgement of the partner each violate. The analysis is therefore assumption-dependent: direct and indirect effects under sequential ignorability, the sensitivity analysis of Imai, Keele and Yamamoto \cite{ImaiKeeleYamamoto2010} over a range of confounding fixed in advance on substantive grounds, and measurement error carried with the confounding by the joint sensitivity analysis of Liu and Wang \cite{LiuWang2021}, the reports' reliabilities from the pilot being its inputs and not a correction on their own.
\item \emph{The two accounts are not exclusive.} Equation~\eqref{eq:cost} does not say by what route mismatch produces effort, and a positive $\lambda$ may describe an effect that runs in part through the gap. A direct effect that shrinks when the gap is controlled is consistent with the cost model; no categorical prediction, zero, the full slope or something between, is made for the residual coefficient; and a direct effect that vanishes at a stated precision would show that the first study cannot find a norm effect beside the gap, not that none exists.
\end{enumerate}
What the first study can establish is whether the mismatch effect is present with the gap controlled, and how much of it the gap carries under the stated assumptions and across the stated sensitivity range. What would separate the mechanisms is a study that manipulates expected empathy directly, by disclosure or by instruction, which is deferred (\autoref{sec:design}).

\subsection{The established setting}

Context-dependent advantages that reverse across conditions are aptitude-by-treatment interactions \cite{Cronbach1957, CronbachSnow1977}, whose disordinal form is the case in which no condition is best for everyone. The norm-setting model is an instance with two specifications: the aptitude is a premeasured profile, not a trait inferred from the outcome, and the treatment is a norm imposed on both partners at once, so that the interaction of interest is between the norm and the \emph{distribution} of cost within the pair. Within that setting the paper's claim is the one of \autoref{sec:intro}: that the distribution is estimable, and separately from the partner effect under an assumption stated in \autoref{sec:design}, by the design that follows.

\section{Study 1: a discriminating design}
\label{sec:design}

The design manipulates the governing rules, holds fixed what can be held fixed of everything else, and measures what cannot. A comparison of ``default conversation'' with ``an explicit protocol'' would change norms, structure, predictability, memory demands and perhaps modality at once, and an improvement under the protocol could be any of them. The study therefore compares two explicit rule sets.

\paragraph{Participants.} Mixed dyads, one autistic and one non-autistic adult, with diagnosis by report and the autistic participants' self-identification recorded. Same-neurotype dyads are a later study (see ``Deferred'' below).

\paragraph{Before any interaction.}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*]
\item Each participant's style profile $s_i$ and competence profile $k_i$ are measured with instruments fixed in the preregistration \cite{Nosek2018}: for style, self-report and a brief performance measure of directness, literalness and turn-yielding preference; for competence, a task of implicature comprehension and a task of following explicit structure, each scored on the scale the requirement is written in.
\item Independent raters, blind to the outcomes, code each rule set's presupposed style $s^{*}_n$ and competence requirement $\theta_n$ from its text, on the same scales.
\item The distance $d$, the weights $w_n$, the outcome measures and the analysis are fixed in the preregistration. Profiles, metric and norm codings chosen after the outcomes are seen would make the model too flexible to fail.
\item The common metric is established, not assumed. Independent coding protects the norm locations from being chosen to fit the outcomes; it does not make a participant's preference score and a coded norm location commensurable. The pilot therefore codes the rule sets on the anchored scales the profile instruments report on, infers the competence thresholds from the rule text on the scale the competence tasks are scored in, and checks the calibration against behaviour: participants' adherence to each rule set and their reported difficulty with it should order with the coded distance, and where they do not the scales are revised before the main study.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{A worked instance.} The notation carries practical choices, and one pair makes them visible. Let the style instrument report directness and literalness on anchored seven-point scales (directness $1$: requests made only by hint; $7$: requests always stated outright), and let the raters place rule set A at $s^{*}_{\mathrm{A}} = (1.5, 2)$ and rule set B at $s^{*}_{\mathrm{B}} = (6.5, 6)$ on the same anchors, from the rules' text. Let the implicature task and the explicit-structure task each be scored $0$ to $10$, and let the raters set rule set A's requirement at $\theta_{\mathrm{A}} = 6$ on implicature, the level of indirectness its hints demand, calibrated in the pilot against the task's items, and rule set B's at $\theta_{\mathrm{B}} = 5$ on explicit structure, each with weight one on its own competence. An autistic participant measured at $s = (6, 6)$, implicature $4$, structure $8$, has style distance $6.0$ to A and $0.5$ to B and a shortfall of $2$ under A and $0$ under B; the non-autistic partner at $s = (2.5, 3)$, implicature $8$, structure $5$, has distances $1.4$ and $5.0$ and no shortfall under either. With $\lambda_s = 0.6$ and $\lambda_k = 0.8$ in workload-score units per scale unit, values the study estimates and the simulation only assumes, the first participant's mismatch is $5.2$ under A and $0.3$ under B, a predicted change of $-4.9$ from A to B, and the partner's is $0.85$ and $3.0$, a change of $+2.15$: the pair's predicted points on the P2 line. Every number in the chain is a choice the pilot has to make good: that a $6$ on a rule's requirement means what a $6$ on the task means, that the raters' $1.5$ and the participant's $2.5$ are a unit apart in the sense the distance assumes, and that the Euclidean distance is the right $d$ for two dimensions that need not count equally.

\paragraph{The manipulation.} Two explicit rule sets differ in the communicator they presuppose and are matched on specified incidental features: length, number of rules, channel, time, and the structure of the task they govern. They are not matched in everything, and the model does not ask them to be. The competence term says that the rule sets draw on different competences; inferring a hint and yielding a turn on an implicit cue make demands that stating a request and yielding in words do not, and those are the differences the model is about. What remains, the learning burden of each rule set and its automaticity after practice, is measured rather than assumed equal: training time to criterion, practice errors and reported effort during practice are recorded for each rule set and serve as the check on the matching, which is treated as a claim the pilot supports or revises. They do not enter the primary analysis as covariates: measured after the participant has met the assigned rules, they can carry part of the adaptation cost the analysis estimates, and adjusting for them would remove that pathway or bias the estimate, the hazard of conditioning on a variable the manipulation affects \cite{MontgomeryNyhanTorres2018}. The primary estimand is the total condition-dependent mismatch effect; how training burden relates to effort is a separate analysis with its own estimand, stated in the preregistration.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Rule set A} encodes indirect, implicature-rich conventions: requests made by hinting, an opening exchange of small talk, turns yielded by implicit cues.
\item \emph{Rule set B} encodes direct, literal conventions: requests stated outright, no obligatory small talk, turns yielded in words.
\end{itemize}
Both are practised to a criterion before the task; adherence to the rules, coded from recordings, is the manipulation check. Which conventions rule set B should encode, and from what sources, is a decision for the preregistration, informed by autistic participants' own accounts of their communication and by the published descriptions of autistic communication preferences; the design does not presume that rule set B is the autistic participants' preferred style, it measures where each participant stands relative to each rule set.

\paragraph{Within dyads.} Each pair works under both rule sets, in counterbalanced order, on two parallel versions of a collaborative task, also counterbalanced. The order is assigned to dyads at random and balanced; a period effect is estimated as the order-by-condition term; differential carryover, which a two-period crossover cannot separate from the order effect \cite{Senn2002}, is guarded against by the parallel task versions and a washout between conditions and is assessed by the adherence coding of the second condition, in which the first rule set's conventions would show if they persisted. The room, lighting and noise are identical across conditions; fatigue is measured after each condition. The pair is its own control: the baseline $a_i$, the partner term $\mu\, d(s_i, s_j)$ and the dyad's shared conditions cancel in the within-person difference \eqref{eq:p2-within}.

\paragraph{Outcomes.} The primary outcome is one measure, fixed in advance: the subjective workload score, the mental-demand and effort items of the NASA Task Load Index \cite{HartStaveland1988} reworded to the conversation just held (how mentally demanding the conversation was; how hard the participant had to work to follow the rules and accomplish the task), on the index's scale, per participant and condition. A behavioural index, the rate of repair initiations and restarts per minute of the participant's speech coded from the recording, is a secondary measure of convergent evidence, not a second primary, so the testing rule has one outcome. Its relation to effort is not assumed: a participant may ask more clarifying questions because that is an effective way to reduce uncertainty, a rule set may make clarification easier or more acceptable, and effortful speech can be fluent, so the pilot establishes what the rate captures, against the workload score and against performance, and whether the per-minute denominator stays comparable when the protocols themselves change speaking time. The raters coding it are blind to the hypotheses, to the participants' group and profiles, and to the condition labels; they are not blind to the conditions themselves, since hints, small talk and spoken turn-yielding are audible, and the design says so rather than claiming a blinding it cannot have. Low reported effort can mean easier coordination or lower engagement, so effort is read with task performance and adherence beside it, and a condition in which all three fall is read as consistent with disengagement rather than with ease. Task performance and rapport, secondary; expected and perceived partner empathy per condition, whose difference is the mediator; rule adherence, the manipulation check; training time, practice errors and practice effort per rule set, the matching check.

\paragraph{Predictions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Primary (P2).} On effort, the rule set interacts with premeasured mismatch within persons: the change in effort from A to B is predicted by the change in $m_i$, with slope one in the model's units and positive in any units. The group-level redistribution of effort is this prediction's aggregate.
\item \emph{$M_\gamma$.} A main effect of rule set only, with no moderation by premeasured mismatch.
\item \emph{$M_\mu$.} No change beyond the main effect, the partner being unchanged.
\item \emph{A norm-dependent double-empathy mechanism.} A change carried by the expected--perceived empathy gap. What the first study can say about it is stated in \autoref{sec:family}; it is not a prediction the primary analysis tests.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Analysis.} The primary analysis is the within-person difference regression of \eqref{eq:p2-within}, the change in the workload score on the change in premeasured style mismatch and competence shortfall, with standard errors clustered by dyad. The preregistered decision rule for \emph{primary success} has three parts: the joint Wald test of $\lambda_s$ and $\lambda_k$ rejects at $5\%$; at least one coefficient is positive by its one-sided $5\%$ test; and neither coefficient contradicts its sign restriction, the one-sided $5\%$ test of $\lambda \ge 0$ rejecting. The first part asks whether premeasured mismatch moderates the change in effort at all, the other two whether it does so in the model's direction with the uncertainty of each estimate respected, since the model permits a coefficient of exactly zero (\autoref{sec:simulation} reports the rule at that boundary). Because success requires the joint rejection, the rule's false-positive rate under the joint null cannot exceed the joint test's. Joint rejection with a sign contradiction is recorded as moderation against the model's direction and counts against the cost model as stated; joint rejection with neither coefficient positive by its one-sided test is recorded as moderation of undetermined direction; neither is success. Evidence for each coefficient separately is secondary, tested one-sided in the predicted direction with the familywise rate held at $5\%$. Levels are analysed in the actor--partner interdependence model \cite{Kenny2006}, participants nested in dyads and conditions within participants, which identifies $\mu$ from between-dyad variation only under an assumption the design does not supply, that the baselines have zero conditional mean given the levels regression's regressors, the measured profile coordinates included as covariates centred within group; with one partner per participant, $a_i' = a_i + \mu\, d(s_i, s_j)$ with $\mu' = 0$ gives the same observations as $a_i$ with $\mu$, so the assumption is what identifies $\mu$ and the supplement shows what its failure does. The four models are compared by their Bayesian information criterion on the exact likelihood stated in the supplement, and the mediation analysis is as stated in \autoref{sec:family}. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} lists what the preregistration fixes.

\paragraph{What would count against the model.} Stated in advance, with the preregistration, and each with the precision it needs, since a nonsignificant estimate establishes no absence:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*, nosep]
\item a manipulation that changes adherence but moves no one's effort, the condition effect and both mismatch coefficients bounded within their smallest effects of interest;
\item a change of effort that premeasured mismatch does not moderate: the joint test not rejecting, and an equivalence test \cite{Lakens2017, Lakens2018} rejecting, for each coefficient, every value beyond its smallest effect of interest; a nonsignificant moderation whose interval still covers the smallest effect of interest is inconclusive, not a disconfirmation;
\item a direct effect of premeasured mismatch bounded within the smallest effect of interest once the change in the empathy gap is controlled, with the sensitivity analysis showing the bound robust across the prespecified range of mediator--outcome confounding.
\end{enumerate}
None of these falsifies the model family, whose parameter space contains $\lambda_s = \lambda_k = 0$; what the first two establish is that mismatch effects of the prespecified size are absent for the studied participants and protocols, which is the result that matters. The smallest effects of interest are provisionally a quarter of the simulated values, $0.15$ for $\lambda_s$ and $0.20$ for $\lambda_k$, a sensitivity setting and not a substantive bound; before the preregistration they are translated, on the fixed scales, into the smallest change in the workload score per unit of premeasured mismatch that would matter. Power to detect those effects and power to establish their absence are separate questions, and \autoref{sec:simulation} reports both. The third condition would show that the first study cannot find a norm effect beside the double-empathy mechanism, and would send the question to the study that manipulates expectations.

\paragraph{Deferred.} Three manipulations go to studies of their own: composition, same-neurotype dyads beside mixed ones, which tests whether the partner term and the norm terms interact and whether the same-neurotype advantage of the first diffusion-chain study \cite{Crompton2020}, absent in its replication \cite{Crompton2025}, appears under a change of norm; disclosure of diagnosis, which manipulates expectations directly and is the study the mediation analysis defers to; and a round robin \cite{KennyLaVoie1984}, each participant with several partners, which identifies the partner term within persons rather than under the between-dyad assumption of \autoref{sec:design}. A first study that crossed norms with any of them would need several times the dyads and would confound its own manipulation checks.

\section{The simulation, in summary}
\label{sec:simulation}

Before any data, the design was run on simulated participants, and the full study is reported in the supplement: the estimators and the exact likelihood, parameter recovery and the power of the decision rule, model recovery, the prediction of a withheld rule set, the reversal arithmetic and a robustness study of seven scenarios. The script, \texttt{norm\_cost\_sim.py} version 1.5, runs with a fixed seed and accompanies the supplement with its output and figures. The settings are $60$ mixed dyads under two rule sets presupposing opposite styles and drawing on different competences, two-dimensional style and competence profiles drawn around overlapping group means, a baseline gap of $0.5$ against a norm-induced gap near $1.5$ in either direction, and a dyad effect shared by the two partners; the generating values are $\lambda_s = 0.6$, $\lambda_k = 0.8$, $\mu = 0.4$ and $\gamma = -0.3$, assumptions chosen for illustration and not estimates.

What the simulation shows, at those settings, is this.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Recovery and power.} Every parameter is recovered without bias and with coverage near nominal; the within-person coefficients are estimated to a tenth of their values, the condition effect has power $81\%$ and the partner term $76\%$ at $60$ dyads. The decision rule succeeds in every study at the generating effects and, at a quarter of them with both coefficients small, in $98.5\%$ of studies at $60$ dyads ($94\%$ with the profiles and codings measured with error), which is the figure the preregistration should quote for the two-coefficient alternative; with one coefficient at its value and the other exactly zero the rule succeeds in $95\%$ and $94\%$, and with one small coefficient alone in $66\%$ and $57\%$, so that a small effect confined to one component needs a larger study. The rule's false-positive rate when no mismatch effect exists is $1.9\%$. The equivalence tests at the smallest effects of interest reach $80\%$ power only above $100$ dyads: a study sized to detect the effects at $60$ dyads is not sized to rule them out (\autoref{fig:power}).
\item \emph{Model recovery.} The Bayesian information criterion on the exact likelihood selects the generating model in $97\%$, $76\%$, $96\%$ and $79\%$ of studies for the four models, its errors almost all the dropping of the partner term; no study generated with the norm terms selected a model without them. A dyad-held-out predictive score over-selects the larger models when the truth is small, the known behaviour of such scores \cite{Stone1977, Shao1993}, and the criterion is the preregistered comparison.
\item \emph{A rule set the model has not seen.} Fitted to rule sets A and B on five sixths of the dyads, the mismatch coefficients predict the relative individual effects of a withheld rule set C for the held-out dyads, from their premeasured profiles alone, to the noise floor ($1.10$ against the rivals' $1.47$, floor $1.10$).
\item \emph{The signature.} \autoref{fig:signature} shows the P2 signature in one simulated study under norm mismatch and under a general protocol benefit: a line of slope near one through the change in premeasured mismatch, the groups moving in opposite directions, against a flat plot with the groups moving together.
\item \emph{Robustness.} The joint test's power, the criterion's selection of a norm model and the within-person estimates survive measurement error in the profiles and codings, halved effects, correlated baselines, unequal residual variances and correlated predictors, singly and together. Two things do not: a rule set's coding error, one draw shared by every participant, spoils the intervals of the condition effect and the competence coefficient, so that the codings must be made by several raters with their disagreement propagated; and baselines correlated with partner distance bias the partner term by more than its value, as the identification argument of \autoref{sec:design} says they must, the profile covariates absorbing almost none of it.
\end{itemize}
The simulation does not show that the effect sizes are realistic, that style and competence can be measured on scales commensurate with the codings of the rule sets, which is the pilot's problem, or anything about the empathy gap, whose analysis depends on assumptions a simulation that built them in could not test.

\section{What existing results constrain}
\label{sec:constraints}

Three bodies of results bear on the design, and each is read from its primary source.

\paragraph{Information transfer in chains.} The first diffusion-chain study found that information passed along same-neurotype chains more effectively than along mixed chains \cite{Crompton2020}; the registered replication with $311$ participants found no main effect of chain type on transfer, for fictional or factual stories, with an unpredicted steeper fall along autistic chains for fictional ones, and rapport effects that depended on role and on being told the chain's neurotype \cite{Crompton2025}. The constraint is twofold: information transfer is not where the cost appeared in that task, and the rapport effects depend on role, which is why effort rather than rapport is the primary outcome here and why rapport gets a model of its own.

\paragraph{Rapport in groups.} In $36$ groups of four, all-autistic, all-non-autistic and three-to-one majorities, there was no overall group-type difference in composite rapport \cite{Foster2025}; all-autistic groups rated their interaction more enjoyable and friendlier than both kinds of mixed group, and autistic participants' ease fell as non-autistic members were added, in item-level results their authors call preliminary. The study did not manipulate norms, so a reading of that pattern through the cost model, the autistic participants' ease falling as the session's conventions shift toward the majority's, is a hypothesis for Study 1 and not a result.

\paragraph{Perception and the double-empathy model.} Non-autistic observers form less favourable first impressions of autistic adults from brief samples, on the basis of presentation rather than content \cite{Sasson2017}, which is one reason the cost of a mixed interaction cannot be read off its outcome alone. The distinct communication strategies model \cite{Calderoli2026} is characterized in \autoref{sec:family}; it supplies the paper's strongest formal rival and the reason the empathy gap is measured.

\section{Outside this paper}
\label{sec:extensions}

Three things the source raises are extensions, and the paper says of each what it would take.

\paragraph{Branch geometry.} The Branch Principle is a hypothesis about the structure of cognitive profiles: at matched resolution, non-autistic profiles form one type, or few, and autistic profiles form several, in the strong form about as distant from each other as from the non-autistic type. Its test fits the same latent-structure models to both groups on the same cognitive-profile measures, a mixture model, an additive tree \cite{SattathTversky1977} and a dimensional model, and compares them by likelihood; the decomposition of autism's phenotypic heterogeneity into classes with distinct genetic programs \cite{Litman2025} is evidence at a different grain. The geometry is independent of the cost model and is not support for mutual advantage, since equal distances do not imply crossed advantages ($b = (1, 1)$ and $c = (1, -1)$ are equidistant from $r = (0, 0)$; $b$ dominates $r$, $c$ and $r$ cross), and linking it to \autoref{prop:reversibility}'s premise needs an argument of its own, for which an asymmetric similarity \cite{Tversky1977} may be the instrument.

\paragraph{Linguistic masking.} The source names a phenomenon a layer below behavioural camouflaging \cite{Hull2017, Hull2019, Pearson2021}: a speaker who learns a cognitive term's public extension case by case over a private intension that differs from the dominant group's, so that the difference shows only on new cases. Its signature, a generalization gradient, is not unique to it, and what would tie it to the mechanism is a private organization of the instances, measured by similarity sorting without the term, predicting the divergence over and above familiarity and confidence; which instruments it would reach is open, since an eight-item alexithymia score shows practically ignorable differential item functioning between the groups \cite{WilliamsGotham2021}. This is a second paper.

\paragraph{Shared absence.} A further hypothesis, that non-reference branches share the absence of the reference's conventions and so find each other's interactions cheaper even where their own preferences differ, is stated with its premises when it is tested, against familiarity, stigma, preference variability and disclosure; it predicts nothing reported here.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the model adds.} The thought experiment of \autoref{sec:redrawing} shows that the traits by which a mixed interaction's difficulty is attributed depend on where the reference stands. The cost model places part of the difficulty in the rules, and makes the placement measurable: the share of the group gap that moves when the rules move, person by person, from profiles measured in advance. That share is what Study 1 estimates, and the model family is what keeps the estimate honest, since a general benefit of explicit rules and a mismatch with the partner are both allowed to carry the data if they can.

\paragraph{The conditional status of the conclusions.} Nothing above establishes that effort tracks mismatch; the paper is built so that the question can be settled. The exact results, the comparative reversibility and the reversal condition, hold of the definitions and the model; the scheme-level symmetry is a conjecture (\autoref{conj:scheme-symmetry}); the cost model is a hypothesis; the Branch Principle is an extension; balance is a conjecture. The reversal condition is a caution to the design: a study that finds the gap reduced by more than the baseline gap has not found a reversal, and a study powered for the reduction is not powered for the crossing.

\paragraph{Limitations the design carries.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Measurement.} The model's content is only as good as the commensurability of the profiles and the norm codings and the choice of $d$ and $w_n$. Fixing them in advance protects the test from being fitted to its outcome, not from a poor choice: mismeasured profiles attenuate the slopes, a shared coding error biases the condition effect with no interval widening to show it (the supplement's robustness study), and a metric that is not in fact common can manufacture an association as easily as hide one. The pilot's calibration is the remedy.
\item \emph{Two rule sets.} Two norms identify the norm terms but not their functional form; a third rule set, as in the simulation's withheld condition, is the natural extension and the strongest test.
\item \emph{Dyads.} The within-person test is clean, but the partner term is identified between dyads, needs $90$ or more pairs at the simulated effect size, and rests on the zero-conditional-mean assumption of \autoref{sec:design}; a round-robin design is the one that identifies it within persons.
\item \emph{Scope.} Both conditions impose explicit instructions, so what the first study measures is adaptation to experimental protocols. Extending its result to the norms of ordinary interaction needs evidence that the protocols represent them, which a comparison of the pilot's adherence codings with recordings of unconstrained conversation can begin to supply.
\item \emph{Generality.} The groups studied are autistic and non-autistic adults, since that is where the question arose and where the literature is; the model is stated for any two groups whose communicative profiles differ, and its application elsewhere is a hypothesis in each case.
\item \emph{Participation.} The rule sets, the profile instruments and the interpretation of effort should be developed with autistic researchers and participants, not only about them; the design's choices that are left open are left open for that reason.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What would advance the account most.} Not a further qualification, but the study itself, preregistered, with its third rule set; and, for the extensions, a cognitive-profile data set at matched resolution on which the branch geometry can be fitted rather than argued.

\section{Preregistration items}
\label{app:prereg}

The preregistration fixes, before any participant is run: the instruments for $s_i$ and $k_i$ and their scales; the two rule sets' texts, their coded $s^{*}_n$, $\theta_n$ and $w_n$, and the rater protocol; the distance $d$; the effort measures, subjective and behavioural, and the expected and perceived empathy reports; the practice-to-criterion rule and the adherence coding; the counterbalancing; the primary outcome, the workload score, with the repair index secondary; the primary analysis of \eqref{eq:p2-within} with dyad-clustered errors, the three-part decision rule of \autoref{sec:design} and how rejections with a sign contradiction or of undetermined direction are recorded, the secondary one-sided tests, and the smallest effects of interest on the fixed scales; the period and carryover terms; the separate analyses of training burden, with their estimands; the actor--partner model for levels with the group-centred profile covariates and its zero-conditional-mean assumption; the model comparison by the Bayesian information criterion on the exact likelihood; the mediation analysis with its sensitivity analysis; the three failure conditions of \autoref{sec:design}; and the sample size, with the intended alternative named: $60$ mixed dyads for the two-coefficient alternative at a quarter of the simulated effects, more if a small effect confined to one component is the alternative of interest (\autoref{sec:simulation}), $90$ if the full family is to be compared, and $120$ if the equivalence tests at the smallest effects of interest are to have $80\%$ power or more, at the simulated effect sizes, which are to be replaced by pilot estimates where available. \section{The source passages}
\label{app:source}

The passages below are quoted as written in the larger work \cite{Aethus}, from the section ``The Aethic Mindset and Autistic Savants'' and its subsection ``Illustrating the Bias of Perspective in Diagnosing Logic'', and from the paragraph ``The decisive fingerprint: it toggles'' of the section ``Aethic Reasoning Against the Modal Structure of Human Language''. The source dates the two principles to a short video the author recorded on June 8, 2024, at 2:39 PM ET.

\paragraph{The Branch Principle, and its motivation.}
\begin{quote}
``Ontologically neurodivergent people are better understood as a collection of categorical branches and `allistic' is a single branch, such that two random branches are typically as different from one another as they each are to a third.''
\end{quote}
\begin{quote}
``A better model for understanding autistic minds than the autistic-allistic binary is more of a tree model, such that allistic people are one branch alone and the rest have been all categorized as `autistic'. Inadvertently speaking, then, this proposes that an autism diagnosis is not actually diagnosing a person's traits to the level of specificity of an allistic person (as would assign them a particular branch) but instead broadly classifies them as `non-allistic' for their lacking allistic traits. In reality, though, each branch comes with a different set of cognitive intuitions, which are separate enough so that two autistic people drawn from two different branches tend to be as different from one another at the cognitive level as either of them are from allistic people.''
\end{quote}
\begin{quote}
``This is meant to be directly analogous to the post-Darwinian tree of life model treating humans as a branch, as opposed to the pre-Darwinian model in which humans and animals are two categories.''
\end{quote}

\paragraph{The Principle of Symmetry, and its intended content.}
\begin{quote}
``You can always redraw the conventional category boundaries in psychology so that allistic and a given neurodivergent branch `trade places phenomenologically', in the sense that the exact behavioral diagnosis, as an Aethus, is made to reverse isomorphically.''
\end{quote}
``As an Aethus'' is glossed at the source as ``a partial information state about that behavioral diagnosis rather than an exhaustive description''. The principle's intended content is given in the same passage with the author's own case as the instance, offered in this paper as motivation only:
\begin{quote}
``What's fundamentally wrong about this is that it fundamentally misunderstands what's actually going on: what's going on is utter, complete symmetry between cognitive branches. That is, for allistic people versus a given branch of autistic people, there will be roughly half-and-half instances across traits for which the autistic person is infantile versus for which the allistic person is infantile. Using myself as an example, at age twenty I still had the emotional maturity of a thirteen year old, but at age thirteen I already had the mathematical maturity of at least a twenty year old. Had my branch been the reference instead, allistic people would look just as profoundly infantile in so many different ways to how I looked in social, emotional, and life skills.''
\end{quote}

\paragraph{The random-partition illustration.}
\begin{quote}
``This is also where categorizing all the non-allistic branches as one `autism' is measurably problematic: you can find an autistic branch for which any trait is infantile, thereby seeming to confirm your prior that `look, autistic people are infantile in this way'. Running the same logic on a random partition where we put half the branches, including the allistic one, in one category while the rest are placed in the other, and we could just as easily misinterpret allistic people as infantile in any conceivable way by finding it in another branch of the categorization and then mistakenly importing it to an assessment of allistic people.''
\end{quote}

\paragraph{The 2024 version of the thought experiment, on ``logical'' and ``nuanced''.} The passage that follows the two principles:
\begin{quote}
``The observation, now, is in how mathematics itself, especially in an educational setting, is commonly called `logical' in the sense of there only being right and wrong answers, whereas the humanities are instead called `nuanced' in the sense that they resist binary `rightness' of answers. What I want to point out now is that this take itself is actually biased to the allistic perspective, which has some intriguing philosophical implications once we start to unpack it. First of all, notice how due to the second bullet point, it is actually universal that anyone learning the most introductory material to a topic of a different intuitive landscape will report the same characterization. In order for a child to learn literature, they must first start by learning the alphabet and the sounds they make. In this sense, like twelfth grade math, kindergarten literature has clear rules---either the letter is written this way or it is not, and either the letter makes this sound or it does not. The fact that kindergarten literature is at an isomorphic introductory level as twelfth grade mathematics is then simply an illustration of how the general population---most of them being of the allistic cognitive branch---hold a vastly higher intuitive capacity for literature over mathematics. So in this sense comparing twelfth grade mathematics to twelfth grade literature is the wrong reference class, hence contributing to this bias of perspective that literature is inherently more nuanced than math.''
\end{quote}

\paragraph{The executive-functioning case and the six traits.} From the author's note of October 24, 2026, 1:48 PM ET, recorded in the larger work beside the Principle of Symmetry:
\begin{quote}
``I myself was talking to a psychiatrist the other day and she asked me the question of `how is your focus, organization, and initiation' for `executive functioning', and basically the understanding is that I'm supposed to say `not great' because in a real-world setting I'm late with the laundry a lot and things like that. Thing is, ontologically what I suspect happened is that you can picture all my executive functioning as having been picked up and moved into straight up inductive reasoning for theory-building and things like that, in which sense all three traits are fantastic, and I'm literally holding a huge network of `good move'-considerations in mind at all times. [\ldots] Basically because of where my executive functioning is situated it falls totally under the radar to standard measures and gets diagnosed as `not there', and the principle of symmetry then specifically says for this that there exists a new way to draw the category boundaries such that the exact allistic and me branches' own diagnoses flip on the matter, or even to arbitrary precision over several matters like as an Aethus augmentation. [\ldots] Literally you name it---pick three positive-existing traits for allistic under executive functioning, and three negative-existing traits for my branch under executive functioning (hence notice how they're just six total traits, not granted `deficient' or not, which would be asinine in terms of killing the actual relativism at play by imposing mainstream-bias on it), and you can literally assign these same traits respectively to any two branches you pick, including the swap case of those very same two! (Not to be confused though with assigning those three traits to respectively allistic and autistic, since my branch principle has the association with branch-swapping as a category error---instead the isomorphism demands you swap them to `single branch versus multiple (specifically remaining collection of) branches' kind of thing).''
\end{quote}

\paragraph{The seat of the cost.} From a footnote of the same section:
\begin{quote}
``[N]otice how many autistic people themselves are described as taking a script to allistic conversations. Once again, notice how if the shoe were on the other foot, for any given non-allistic cognition branch allistic people practicing its topics would be interpretable as using a script.''
\end{quote}

\paragraph{The toggle.}
\begin{quote}
``One further fingerprint is of a different kind, and it is decisive against the strongest rival reading: that the convention is not a convention at all but a fixed cognitive limitation. A limitation does not toggle. The switching just described---an adult moving, on demand, between the attached picture and the conventional one within a single day, by context---is a \emph{dissociability} result: the convention is a mode the platform runs, selectable, not a ceiling the platform imposes.''
\end{quote}
