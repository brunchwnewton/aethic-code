The Dark Era

The weight paradigm, measured at equal mass

The recalibration page ranks animals of every size. This one holds size fixed and asks a question the ranking cannot: at the same body mass, does the crowd already credit a theropod's body plan over a mammal's? The crowd's own votes answer it, and the anatomy model shows what changes when the credit is added.

The question: the recalibration page multiplies every dinosaur by one anatomy factor, an elicited sensitivity model rather than something the votes identify. This page checks the premise behind it, that the crowd scores by weight. If the crowd already credited body plans, a theropod would outscore a mammal of the same mass and the ratio above would sit above parity. It doesn't: across the masses where both groups have data, the crowd rates a carnivorous theropod at roughly half to four-fifths of an equal-mass mammal, with a bootstrap band that reaches parity but not much beyond it. That is what a weight paradigm looks like in the votes, and it is consistent with the hypothesis without proving it.

How it is computed: each dot is an animal with a body mass and the same crowd Bradley–Terry baseline the recalibration page uses (about 4,494 matchups, refitted in October 2026, African Lion = 100). Each group gets a power law fitted here in the browser by median regression in log–log space, robust to outlier votes; the ratio is the theropod fit divided by the mammal fit at each mass, and its band comes from 300 bootstrap resamples of both groups. The anatomy factor multiplies every theropod score by the same number, so it lifts the ratio without changing its shape. The generic Lion and Tiger entries are left out, since the crowd scores those culturally iconic names well above their own populations (the paper's cultural-inflation section). Mammals below 1 kg are excluded from the fit, so the curves are extrapolated below 1 kg and drawn dashed there, with the region hatched.

What the chart does not show: the Dark Era's predation ran from larger animals to smaller ones, and an equal-mass comparison cannot measure it; the 100 g marker shows where our ancestors sat, two orders of magnitude below the mammal data, where every number is extrapolation. The ratio also depends on which theropods count: with the gliding Yi qi, the oviraptorid Citipati and the ornithomimids included ("All"), the small end rises and the large end falls. Mass estimates for extinct animals, the crowd's composition and the small samples are uncertainties the bootstrap band does not capture.
