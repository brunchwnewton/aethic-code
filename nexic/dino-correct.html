How this works: Power ratings are fitted from >2,000 crowd-sourced matchup votes using a Bradley-Terry model. The crowd rates animals through a weight paradigm — heavier animals score higher. But Mesozoic animals fought with body plans refined over 160 million years: sickle claws, crushing jaws, horns, clubs, and armor purpose-built for killing.

The anatomy correction rescales dinosaurs by ~12.7× so that a Dakotaraptor overpowers a big cat the way a lion overpowers an unarmed human. Mesozoic non-dinosaurs get √ correction (~3.6×). All scores are normalized where Big Cat ≡ 100.

On the calibration: The anatomy correction is my hypothesis, constrained so that Dakotaraptor/Lion = Lion/unarmed Human — that is, equal log-spacing, or equivalently, the same multiplicative ratio. This is not an arbitrary choice: it is the point at which the anatomical difference is already most viscerally intuitive to us. Everyone understands that a big cat takes down an unarmed human in seconds, not because the cat is heavier, but because its claws, jaws, and reflexes are purpose-built weapons against which bare skin and flat teeth are simply not viable. The thesis is that a Dakotaraptor's sickle claws, jaw (it is looking down at the tiger from above), and startling agility despite its size do the same to a big cat: the fight is over before it begins, for exactly the same anatomical reasons, scaled up one rung. Consider the jaws alone, fully fleshed out as they actually were in life: a meter-long wall of straight weaponry, serrated teeth lining an enormous lever arm. Think about how much arm length alone affects a tennis serve. The jaw is the arm length. A crocodile is already terrifying, and its jaws work from below with limited leverage. A Dakotaraptor swings that lever from above, with the full mechanics of its posture behind it. That difference in leverage is basically the 12× correction in a single image. This is why the fight is exactly like tiger vs. human. I don't care if the tiger is swatting, or fleeing, or biting, the Dakotaraptor wraps its jaws around the tiger's entire skull uninhibited.

The broader claim: The Mesozoic was an unrecognizable planet. Once theropod dinosaurs evolved, they contaminated the environment in a way that is difficult to overstate. Body plans refined over 160 million years into killing machines of a kind the Cenozoic simply never reproduced. Think of it less as "dinosaurs were big" and more like an alien invasion that already happened: the land was overrun, and by any measure of evolutionary fitness, mammals had no answer. Our ancestors survived the Dark Era (the formal term from my framework) not by competing with theropods but by hiding from them: nocturnal, burrowing, small. The entire mammalian cognitive architecture, from the limbic system to the structure of our senses, was forged under that predation pressure.

And then, somehow, it ended. The Chicxulub impactor struck at precisely the angle, velocity, and location needed to terminate the theropod regime while preserving the mammalian lineages that would eventually produce us. Assemble the hundred most brilliant strategists in human history, grant them any technology, and ask them to solve this problem. They would almost certainly fail. The variables are too numerous, the margins too fine. That the specific impact configuration managed to end this runaway system of anatomical escalation is, in my framework, capital-M Miraculous: not a coincidence, but the kind of event whose staggering improbability is constitutive of, rather than incidental to, our existence as observers.

Also, p.s., somebody really needs to make a handheld-style CGI video of a Dakotaraptor going against a tiger from within this paradigmatic view of mine. It's going to be horrifying to watch (think dino Game of Thrones) but it sure will get the point across! Seriously though, think about what seeing one saltwater crocodile come out of the water online does to your perception. Nobody has had that moment with dinosaurs yet, which is why people haven't calibrated what we're dealing with here. If you do the math, the fight between a Dakotaraptor and a tiger is less a fight and more a slaughter. Unpleasant as all hell, but it really makes you appreciate what our ancestors sacrificed to get us here!
